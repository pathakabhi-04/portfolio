## The problem

Skin-lesion classifiers score well on the dataset they were trained on, then fail on photos from other sources. On 792 outside clinical photos, only 57.9% of melanomas were sent on to a clinician.

## What I built

An eight-stage pipeline behind a fixed JSON output format and a FastAPI endpoint, with a p50 latency of 0.52s on CPU:

- Image-quality gating and routing
- Lesion detection and segmentation
- A 6-class classifier plus a referral head
- Grad-CAM explainability and uncertainty estimation
- Change-over-time tracking between visits

A RAG layer on top explains the results in plain language.

## The hard part: finding out why it failed

I ran four experiments, each with its pass or fail rule set in advance. None met the 0.80 AUC bar.

| Experiment | AUC |
| --- | --- |
| Linear probe | 0.73 |
| Backbone fine-tune on cloud GPUs | 0.63–0.67 |
| Ruling out routing and detection as causes | — |
| Multi-domain training head | 0.63–0.64 |

Together they showed the problem was the data, not the model.

Along the way I caught two problems that would have inflated the results:

- A patient leak in a public dataset. 25 of 132 test images shared a patient with training.
- A hidden bug where turning off one stage quietly dropped 45% of inputs and made the score look better than it was.

## Outcome

I shipped a narrower product that tracks change over time and never gives a diagnosis. I also designed a consent-gated data-collection system to close the gap:

- Hand-writable checksummed IDs that catch every single-character typo and swapped pair. My first version missed 5% of typos, and the test suite caught it.
- A revocation path, and exports that keep each patient's images together.

The project has 360 automated tests.
